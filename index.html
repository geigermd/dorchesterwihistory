Dorchester, WisconsinA railroad town and its people, 1873 to today

Dorchester began as a stop on the Wisconsin Central Railway, cut through the northwoods in the early 1870s and named, like its neighbors, after a town near Boston.

Danish, Norwegian, German and Bohemian families followed the rails. They cleared the land, ran sawmills and saloons along Front Street, and built a village that has survived blizzards, fire and school consolidation. This page tells that story through the artifacts at the Dorchester Historical Center and photographs saved by local families.

1871 – 1886

The rails come north

The Wisconsin Central broke ground at West Menasha on June 5, 1871, and reached Stevens Point that November. In 1872 the line was finished to a new stop named Colby, after Charles L. Colby of Boston, whose company held the contract to build the road to Ashland.

On April 15, 1873, crews began laying rail north from Colby. Stations along the way were named for Massachusetts towns: Medford, Chelsea, Dedham, Walden, Worcester, and Dorchester, then a small community outside Boston. The historical center dates the railroad's arrival in Dorchester to April 18, 1873.

The first iron rails twisted out of shape in the northern weather and were replaced with steel in 1886. The first depot was an old Wisconsin Central boxcar set beside the tracks. It burned in 1896.

1870s – 1880s

Homesteads and first families

Settlers claimed land on both sides of the tracks in what was then the Town of Mayville. A hand-researched map by Linda (Johnson) Mertens shows each homestead claim from 1870 to 1875. Daniel O. Miltimore (1838–1901) is remembered at the historical center as the "First Citizen of Dorchester."

  • Aug. 1877Andrew Sorenson arrives from Aarhus, Denmark. His father buys "the first parcel of railroad land sold in this vicinity," a mile and a half west of the village. Andrew and his brother go into the meat market business.
  • c. 1883Dr. H. M. Nedry, "one of the first doctors of Dorchester country," begins a practice he keeps until 1910.
  • 1886H. LaBossier opens a general store. His hall later hosts the first village election.
  • 1888A. F. F. Jensen builds a two-story, 26-by-60-foot general store with a warehouse beside it.
1880s – 1900s

Lumber, threshing and a German community

The forest paid the bills. The best timber stood close to the rail line, so horse teams could haul logs cheaply to the mills and the cars. A stencil reading "Van Dusen & Co., Shaded A, Dorchester, Wis." marked the town's lumber, and shaving benches like the 1883 schnitzelbank at the historical center turned out roof shingles and ax handles. Paulson's sawmill appears in photos from 1905 to 1912.

Many newcomers were German-speaking. A trunk at the historical center is addressed by hand to Johann Jantsch in "Nord Amerika." In September 1889 the German Farmers Aid Society held a harvest festival with a full brass band. By 1898 The New Era carried ads for the Dorchester Roller Mills and its "Monogram Flour."

1901

Dorchester becomes a village

On September 28, 1901, the Clark County Circuit Court ordered the village incorporated, and voters approved it on October 22. The election inspectors then set the first village election for November 12, 1901, from ten in the morning to four in the afternoon at LaBossier's Hall.

"…a President, six trustees, a clerk, a treasurer, a supervisor, an assessor, a constable and two justices of the Peace."

Andrew Sorenson served as the village's first treasurer, and later as street commissioner and constable for twenty years.

1900s – 1910s

Front Street years

Front Street ran along the tracks, unpaved and lined with saloons, stores and hotels: Kronschnabl's and Fred Marten's saloons, the "Jerk Water Saloon and Dance Hall," the Dorchester House, August Miller's hotel, Esser's Hall and an Opera House. The Dorchester State Bank opened in 1909 and the Co-op store by 1912. Congregations built Salem Church, the "Little White Church" and St. Peter's Lutheran.

The high school held its 1913 commencement at the Opera House for a class of seven. A three-story brick high school stood by 1916.

1917 – 1923

The big snow and the big fire

On March 18, 1917, a blizzard buried a Soo Line locomotive at Dorchester. A crew of men and boys dug out the cut by hand. One photographer captioned it "The boys on the Soo Line, not in the trenches," a nod to the war overseas.

Six years later came what the paper called "the most serious fire since 1895." It started around midnight in Sauter's bowling alley and spread through about fifteen buildings. Dorchester's own fire engine broke its drive shaft, and Abbotsford and Medford sent chemical engines. Paulson's Garage, the Kronschnabl post office, Homsted's drugstore, the Fuchsgruber and Holz Bros. stores and the Bauernfeind home were lost, about $150,000 in damage. Several owners rebuilt.

1920s – 1930s

Between the wars

  • 1916–1925Herman Kronschnabl serves as postmaster and publishes the Dorchester Clarion, the weekly paper the town kept for decades.
  • 1919–1937The Tri-City Band of Abbotsford, Colby and Dorchester plays, including three years as the Clark County Fair band.
  • 1920Electricity comes to Dorchester. Families retire their oil lamps.
  • 1929The Central Canning Co. runs a factory in town. Pea canning continues into the late 1930s.
  • 1935Banded sandstone from the local quarry builds the Seidel Bros. D-X service station, which also served malts and fast food.
  • 1937The first Dorchester Days. Weekly Wednesday-night cash drawings were backed by about 25 businesses, including Werner's Grocery, Sauter Brothers, Paulson Garage, the Block Hotel, Weix Drug and Genrich Implement.
  • 1938The village water tower goes up.
1913 – 1962

The Dorchester Red Devils

Dorchester High School held a commencement as early as 1913, and its full four-year program began with the class of 1925. Boys played basketball, baseball, softball, kittenball, horseshoes, tennis, track and six-man touch football. Girls played basketball, kittenball, tennis and volleyball until 1945.

  • 1942The Red Devils win the Three C Conference tournament, beating Loyal in the playoff.
  • 1948–49A 16–5 season, unbeaten at home, and a win at the Athens tournament.
  • 1949–50District champions again under coach Warren Otto. New jackets arrived by train during the first half of the last game and were worn for the second-half warm-up.
  • 1962The last Dorchester High School class graduates. From 1963, Dorchester students graduate from Dor-Abby High School with Abbotsford.
1940s – 1960s

Mid-century Dorchester

The American Legion post was named Leach-Paulson Post 517 for two Dorchester High School graduates of 1940 who died in World War II, Sgt. Kenneth Leach and Pvt. Donald Paulson. Its auxiliary organized in March 1948, and a new Legion Hall opened in 1949.

A new post office opened in 1942. Ed Werner's Clover Farm store sold groceries and meats. The Dorchester Furniture Co. made coffee and end tables in the 1950s. The Lions Club ran a Halloween parade with movies, treats and prizes. A new grade school and a new Co-op feed mill opened in 1960, and the Laabs-Reynolds factory by 1968.

1971 – 1980

A lake, a park and a hundred years

Lake Dorchester was created around 1971, and volunteers built the park beside it. By 1980 it had a 15-acre stocked lake, a ball diamond, a swimming pool, tennis courts, playground equipment and two shelter houses. "The park is a real community undertaking," longtime volunteer Pinter told the Times Review. "Very few persons say no when asked to help do some work there." Dorchester Days grew around a state championship tractor pull.

On June 16, 1973, the village celebrated its centennial with a parade of horse teams, antique cars and floats. Floats came from the Dorchester Cheese Co., Dorchester State Bank, Meyer Lumber, Krueger Super Market, Sauter Bros., Kalepp Fish Farm, Schwoch Insurance, Mertens Garage, local taverns and churches, the 4-H, the Scouts, the American Legion and the Lions. Diane Royek rode as Dorchester Queen.

1990 – today

Recent years

  • 1990The Legion post counts five living World War I veterans, aged 91 to 96: Otto Genrich, Henry Genrich, Bill Jantsch, Walter Tischendorf and Dr. A. W. Schief.
  • 1991A new bank building opens.
  • 1995Governor Tommy Thompson presents a $48,500 transportation grant to the village. Jim Jantsch serves as village president from 1995 to 2003.
  • 2002The Weyerhaeuser Company Foundation, on behalf of Northwest Hardwoods in Dorchester, gives $11,500 for emergency sirens at the north and south edges of the village.
  • TodayThe Dorchester Historical Center keeps the town's artifacts, from rail spikes and milk cans to Bill Jantsch's centennial hat.

  • Photo dates come from the family collection and are sometimes approximate.
