Clark County, Wisconsin. A railroad village, its people, and the pictures they kept.

Dorchester started as a stop on the Wisconsin Central Railway, which was pushed north through the woods in the early 1870s. The railroad named its new stations after towns around Boston, and this one got the name of a small community that is now part of that city.

The village sits right on the 45th parallel, on high ground between the Eau Pleine River to the east and the Poplar River to the west. Rain on one side of town runs off toward the Wisconsin River, and rain on the other side toward the Black. Nearly all of Dorchester is in Clark County, though a sliver reaches into Marathon County.

Danish, Norwegian, German and Bohemian families came on the train and stayed. They cleared stump land, worked in the sawmills, ran the stores and saloons on Front Street, and kept the village going through blizzards, fires and the closing of its high school. 1871 to 1886

The rails come north

The Wisconsin Central broke ground at West Menasha on June 5, 1871, and reached Stevens Point that November. In 1872 the line was finished to a new stop called Colby, named for Charles L. Colby of Boston, whose company held the contract to build the road to Ashland.

Crews began laying rail north from Colby on April 15, 1873. The stations along the way took Massachusetts names: Medford, Chelsea, Dedham, Walden, Worcester and Dorchester. The historical center dates the railroad's arrival in Dorchester to April 18, 1873.

The first iron rails twisted out of shape in the northern weather and were replaced with steel in 1886. For a depot, the railroad set an old boxcar beside the track. It burned in 1896.

1870s and 1880s

Homesteads and first families

Settlers took up land on both sides of the tracks in what was then the Town of Mayville. Linda (Johnson) Mertens later mapped every homestead claim from 1870 to 1875. Daniel O. Miltimore (1838–1901) is remembered at the historical center as the "First Citizen of Dorchester."

1874 The village is platted on Wisconsin Central land. Miltimore arrives that summer and builds the first building in town, a store, and serves as postmaster and as the railroad's station and land agent until 1882. S. Hugaboom of Oshkosh opens the first hotel. Miltimore later moves to Los Angeles. In February 1876 the Stevens Point Journal calls him "the pioneer of Dorchester," its postmaster and merchant, and reports "a thriving village" whose school averages seventy-five pupils.

1877 Andrew Sorenson arrives from Aarhus, Denmark. His father buys "the first parcel of railroad land sold in this vicinity," a mile and a half west of the village.

1881 Frank and Mary Skerbeck, a Bohemian circus family, step off the train with four children to take up 120 acres of forest land.

c. 1883 Dr. H. M. Nedry, "one of the first doctors of Dorchester country," begins a practice he keeps until 1910.

1886 H. LaBossier opens a general store. The Star Hotel goes up on South Front Street.

1888 A. F. F. Jensen builds a two-story, 26-by-60-foot general store.

The 1895 Wisconsin state census went house by house through the Town of Mayville, which still included Dorchester since the village wasn't incorporated until 1901. The town clerk, Paul Blanc, counted 275 households and 1,398 people. The census recorded only where each person was born, so children born here to German, Danish or Norwegian parents are counted under the United States. Familiar names fill the pages: LeClaire, Paulson, Sorenson, Jensen, Mehner, Genrich, Gutwasser, Miltimore, Distelhorst and Kronschnabl. The same census lists 18 Civil War veterans living in Mayville, most getting their mail at Dorchester.

Who owned the land, 1893

An 1893 county atlas mapped every farm in the Town of Mayville and printed the owner's name on it. Dorchester is the shaded block on the railroad in Section 13, with the Paulsons, LeClairs, Sorensons, Miltimores, Jensens and Malchows all around it. The Wisconsin Central runs down the right side to Abbotsford, and a second line crosses the bottom toward Curtiss. Tap the map to see it larger.

1880s to 1900s

Lumber and threshing

The woods paid the bills. The best timber stood close to the track, so horse teams could haul logs cheaply to the mills and the rail cars. Around 1881 the village had about 400 people, and most of them worked for the sawmill. The first mill, the Evans mill, burned in 1880. O. D. Van Dusen built a new one on the same spot, east of the tracks on ground that is now Dorchester Park, and had it running by early 1881. Later came the Ellingson Lumber Co. mill, which Ole Paulson came to manage in 1897 and bought outright in 1905. A stencil reading "Van Dusen & Co., Shaded A, Dorchester, Wis." marked its lumber. Shaving benches like the 1883 schnitzelbank at the historical center turned out shingles and ax handles.

Many of the newcomers spoke German. A trunk at the historical center is addressed by hand to Johann Jantsch in "Nord Amerika." In September 1889 the German Farmers Aid Society held a harvest festival with a full brass band. By 1898 The New Era carried ads for the Dorchester Roller Mills and its "Monogram Flour."

1901

Dorchester becomes a village

That summer the village was surveyed and a census counted 471 people. On September 28, 1901, the Clark County Circuit Court ordered the village incorporated, and the voters approved it on October 22. The election inspectors set the first village election for November 12, from ten in the morning until four in the afternoon, at LaBossier's Hall. Voters were to choose

"…a President, six trustees, a clerk, a treasurer, a supervisor, an assessor, a constable and two justices of the Peace."

Andrew Sorenson was the first village treasurer. He later served twenty years as street commissioner and constable.

1900s and 1910s

Front Street

Front Street ran along the tracks, unpaved, lined with saloons, stores and hotels. There were Kronschnabl's and Fred Marten's saloons, the "Jerk Water Saloon and Dance Hall," the Star Hotel, the Dorchester House, August Miller's hotel, Fred Distelhorst's brick store, Esser's Hall and an Opera House. The Dorchester State Bank opened in 1909 and the Co-op store by 1912. In 1919 a crew hauled in wagonloads of stone to level the street.

Churches came early. St. Peter's Lutheran organized in 1880, worshipped in a log church from 1883 and dedicated its brick church on November 22, 1903. Salem Evangelical organized in 1882. St. Louis Catholic, a mission since the late 1870s, built a brick church in 1898 and a new one in 1930. The German Evangelical Peace congregation followed in 1898. There was also the "Little White Church." The high school held its 1913 commencement in the Opera House for a class of seven, and a three-story brick high school stood by 1916. The town's three cemeteries were started by its churches. The pioneers called them "Gottes Acker," God's Acres. The Norwegian Lutherans bought land for theirs in October 1888 for $30. The German Evangelical congregation began Peace Cemetery, today North Dorchester Memorial. The largest, South Memorial, was begun jointly by the Catholic, Baptist and Lutheran churches, became Union Cemetery and then Dorchester Memorial Park in 1927. A fire in the 1920s destroyed the early cemetery records, so the first surviving entries date from January 1927.

In 1912 a photographer climbed to the top of the mill and took the whole village in three frames, sold as a "Birdseye View of Dorchester." Put back together, they show the town from the railroad yard on the left, down Front Street, to the farm fields on the right.

A Clark County history from around 1915 says the village "at one time was an important mill town" and now prospered "as the center of a rich farming community." Its business directory reads like a walk down Front Street:

1917 to 1931

The big snow and the big fires

On March 18, 1917, a blizzard buried a Soo Line locomotive at Dorchester, and men and boys from town dug out the cut by hand. Someone wrote on one of the pictures, "The boys on the Soo Line, not in the trenches."

Fire was an old enemy. In 1888 one burned nearly every business in town, and in 1897 another took a whole block. Then, on the night of Tuesday, May 1, 1923, came what the Weekly Clarion called "the most serious fire since 1895." It started around midnight in Sauter's bowling alley and spread through about fifteen buildings. Dorchester's own fire engine broke its drive shaft, and Abbotsford and Medford sent chemical engines. Paulson's Garage, the Kronschnabl post office, Homsted's drugstore, the Fuchsgruber and Holz Bros. stores, Joe Bauernfeind's blacksmith shop and home, Dr. Foley's office and the Clarion's own office were lost, about $150,000 in all. It leveled the south end of Front Street. Several owners rebuilt. Another fire on May 17, 1929, which started in the Heuser building and took four businesses, was stopped at the brick wall of the Dorchester State Bank.

On February 13, 1931, crowds watched the Joe Kronschnabl building burn. It held the family's home and, in front, a saloon and bowling alley run by Carl Mertens. Two chemical trucks held the fire for an hour, but "the old relic of a fire engine refused to operate," and help came from Colby. Mertens reopened in the garage next door.

1920s and 1930s

Between the wars

1916–25 Herman Kronschnabl is postmaster and publishes the Dorchester Clarion, founded around 1900 by Ray Hugoboom, the weekly paper the town kept for decades.

1919–37 The Tri-City Band of Abbotsford, Colby and Dorchester plays, including three years as the Clark County Fair band.

1920 Electricity reaches Dorchester. The oil lamps go on the shelf.

1929 The Central Canning Co. runs a factory in town. Peas are still being canned in the late 1930s.

1935 Sandstone from the local quarry goes into the Seidel Bros. D-X service station, which also served malts and burgers.

1937 The first Dorchester Days. About 25 businesses back weekly Wednesday-night cash drawings, among them Werner's Grocery, Sauter Brothers, Paulson Garage, the Block Hotel, Weix Drug and Genrich Implement.

1938 The village water tower goes up.

From the air, August 13, 1938

In the late 1930s the federal government photographed every farm in Wisconsin from the air. Dorchester's frame was shot on August 13, 1938. The village sits in the lower right corner, where County A crosses the railroad. Front Street runs along the tracks, the houses fill a few blocks south of County A, and a cluster of larger buildings, likely the mills and warehouses, stands just east of the tracks. The photo stops at the frame edge, so the south end of town is cut off. Everything around it is farm fields and woodlots, the land that was stump country fifty years before. 1925 to 1962

The Red Devils

Dorchester High School held a commencement as early as 1913, and its full four-year course began with the class of 1925. Boys played basketball, baseball, softball, kittenball, horseshoes, tennis, track and six-man touch football. Girls played basketball, kittenball, tennis and volleyball until 1945.

1942 The Red Devils win the Three C Conference tournament, beating Loyal in the playoff.

1948–49 Sixteen wins and five losses, unbeaten at home, and the Athens tournament title.

1949–50 District champions again under coach Warren Otto. The new jackets came in on the train during the first half of the last game and were worn for the second-half warm-up.

1962 The last Dorchester High School class graduates. From 1963 on, Dorchester students graduate from Dor-Abby High School with Abbotsford.

1940s to 1960s

Mid-century

Dorchester has sent its share to war. Its Civil War veterans formed the Howard F. Pruyn Post No. 168 of the Grand Army of the Republic around 1885, and the 1895 census still counted 18 of them in the township. In World War I, Pvt. Edward Knaack was killed in action in the Argonne on October 18, 1918, and Pvt. Albert Knaack Jr. died in service nine days earlier. After World War II, veterans organized in August 1946, and their post was chartered in 1947 as Leach-Paulson Post 517. It honors three men: Pvt. Donald Paulson, killed in Germany in January 1945; Kenneth Leach, killed in a B-17 training crash in Texas in 1943; and his brother, Pfc. Elmer Leach, a tank crewman killed in France in June 1944. The auxiliary organized in March 1948, and a new Legion Hall opened in 1949.

A new post office opened in 1942. Ed Werner's Clover Farm store sold groceries and meat. The Dorchester Furniture Co. made coffee and end tables in the 1950s. The Lions Club put on a Halloween parade with movies, treats and prizes. A new grade school and a new Co-op feed mill opened in 1960, and the Laabs-Reynolds factory was running by 1968.

In September 1959 fire took Carlson's Garage, a building more than 75 years old that had once been Dorchester's public school. It had been moved after the newer school went up around 1916 and spent decades as Edgar Paulson's garage. In 1965 the village took bids to replace its old frame village hall, "erected about a half century ago." The new municipal building, about $69,000, opened in January 1966 with room for the fire department that served the north halves of Mayville and Holton towns.

1968 to 1980

A lake, a park and a hundred years

In 1968 the village laid out a 45-acre recreational park east of town, dug out a 15-acre lake and built a dam, on the same ground where the Van Dusen sawmill had stood. Volunteers did much of the work. By 1980 it had a 15-acre stocked lake, a ball diamond, a swimming pool, tennis courts, playground equipment and two shelter houses. "The park is a real community undertaking," longtime volunteer Pinter told the Times Review. "Very few persons say no when asked to help do some work there." Dorchester Days grew up around a state championship tractor pull.

On June 16, 1973, the village marked its hundredth year with a parade of horse teams, old cars and floats from the Dorchester Cheese Co., the bank, Meyer Lumber, Krueger Super Market, Sauter Bros., Kalepp Fish Farm, Schwoch Insurance, Mertens Garage, the taverns and churches, the 4-H, the Scouts, the Legion and the Lions. Diane Royek rode as Dorchester Queen.

1990 to today

Recent years

1990 The Legion post counts five living World War I veterans, aged 91 to 96: Otto Genrich, Henry Genrich, Bill Jantsch, Walter Tischendorf and Dr. A. W. Schief.

1991 A new bank building opens. Ludwig Foods, a supermarket run by Ron and Mark Ludwig, becomes the first business in the new industrial park and gives the village a grocery store again after Krueger's market closed.

1995 Governor Tommy Thompson brings a $48,500 transportation grant. Jim Jantsch serves as village president from 1995 to 2003.

2002 Ground is broken for a water treatment plant on June 28. The Weyerhaeuser Company Foundation, for Northwest Hardwoods in Dorchester, gives $11,500 for emergency sirens.

2005 Jim Jantsch publishes Dorchester: Small in Size, Big in History and Happenings.

2008 Lawrence Boxrucker, a Navy fireman who grew up on a farm outside Dorchester and was killed aboard the USS Oklahoma at Pearl Harbor, is identified almost 67 years later, announced September 4, 2008. Two days later he is buried with military honors at Dorchester Memorial Cemetery. His twin brother, Louis, who joined the Navy with him, had lived out his life in Dorchester.

2020 The census counts 849 people, more than at any point in the lumber days. Nearly all of them live on the Clark County side; only a small corner of the village reaches into Marathon County.

Today Dorchester Days still comes around every June, built around the Wisconsin State Championship Tractor Pull, with a truck pull, a carnival, softball, volleyball and cornhole tournaments, a kiddie parade, street dances and a Sunday chicken dinner.

Faces from the album

People of Dorchester

Many of the photographs on this page were taken by Joe Weber, who set up as "Jos. C. Weber, Photographer, Dorchester Wis." around 1911. He carried mail on Rural Route 2 from 1917 until he left for the Army in July 1918, later served as fire chief, and was still in the pictures in the 1970s.

The Skerbecks came the furthest. Frank Skerbeck was a Bohemian sword swallower and high-wire walker whose family circus had toured Europe. After it went broke, a land agent sold him on northern Wisconsin, and in 1881 the family got off the train at Dorchester. Their 120 acres turned out to be thick forest, so they sold the timber to the Dorchester sawmill, and Frank trained the children in the barn. For about 25 years they played vaudeville in winter and toured under canvas in summer, and for a few years they joined Medford's Ephraim Williams in the "Consolidated American and German Railroad Shows." They practiced on what neighbors called the "Old Blue Farm" in Deer Creek township, took out their first tent show in 1884, and opened with Williams at Medford on July 21, 1885, with ten wagons and a twelve-piece band. The family business never stopped. It became the Skerbeck carnival, later based in Escanaba, Michigan, and is still run by Frank's descendants, now the sixth generation. Their story is told in Joan M. Jensen's "The Skerbeck Circus," Voyageur magazine, 2008.
